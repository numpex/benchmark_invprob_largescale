Cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) images frozen biological samples by
recording a tilt series: a few dozen projections of the same specimen, each
taken at a different angle and at a very low electron dose. Reconstructing
the volume from these projections yields a 3D map that is dominated by noise,
and there is no clean reference to train a denoiser against. The denoiser is
therefore trained on the measured data itself, in a self-supervised way, and
the training has to run on full-size volumes.

That is where the cost comes from. A volume of 1024×512×1024 voxels holds 537
million voxels, 2.1 GB in fp32 before any network has been applied. A 3D
convolutional network produces feature maps with as many spatial positions as
the volume, multiplied by the number of channels, and the backward pass needs
them. One training step on such a volume takes minutes on four GPUs. This
page measures what shortens that step, what makes it fit in memory, and how it
should be spread over GPUs.

## The method being trained

The tilt series is split into two half-sets with independent noise, $y_e$ and
$y_o$. Each is reconstructed by filtered back-projection (FBP), and a 3D U-Net
$f_\theta$ denoises both reconstructions, giving $x_e$ and $x_o$. Each result
is scored against the other half:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{obs}} &= \lVert A_o x_e - y_o \rVert^2 + \lVert A_e x_o - y_e \rVert^2,\\
\mathcal{L}_{\text{eq}} &= \lVert f_\theta(\mathrm{FBP}_e(A_e T x_e)) - T x_o \rVert^2
  + \lVert f_\theta(\mathrm{FBP}_o(A_o T x_o)) - T x_e \rVert^2,
\end{aligned}
$$

with $A$ the projection operators, $T$ a random rotation and
$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{obs}} + \lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{eq}}$
($\lambda = 2$ in all runs here).
A step therefore runs $f_\theta$ four times on the full volume, all
differentiated, each time on overlapping tiles distributed over the GPUs.

The runs on this page use a synthetic tilt series modelled on EMPIAR-11830: a
1024×512×1024 volume and 41 tilt angles over ±60°. They ran on Adastra, with
four AMD MI300A APUs per node. In each APU, the CPU and the GPU share
**128 GB** of memory. The CPU side uses about 10 GB at peak in these runs, so
the 128 GB is treated below as the memory of the GPU. Times are the
forward + backward time of a training step, as the median of steps 3 to 5;
the first two steps are warm-up and are discarded. Memory is the peak
per-GPU allocation of forward and backward. Parallel efficiency is
$100 \times t_4 \times 4 / (t_N \times N)$, against the 4-GPU run.

## Precision, compilation and distributed physics: where the step time goes

In fp32, one step takes six minutes on four GPUs, and each sweep on this page
repeats it for several steps and configurations. The step cost is therefore
the first thing to reduce. Each row below changes one setting against the
baseline: fp32, no compilation, and the physics (the tomography operator)
replicated on every rank. With distributed physics, the operator is split by
tilt angle across the ranks.

**Precision sets the step time.** bf16 alone makes the step 5.3× faster, from
363 s to 69 s (+429%). Almost all of the gain is in the backward pass, which
drops from 316 s to 51 s: the step is dominated by the 3D convolutions of the
denoiser, and bf16 is what makes them cheap.

The other two settings are small in fp32: distributed physics gives 1.2%
(363.1 s to 358.7 s) and compilation 0.3%. With the convolutions in fp32,
nothing else in the step is large enough to matter. Once bf16 has reduced
them, compilation and distributed physics together save a further 7.5% (68.6 s
to 63.8 s), mostly in the forward pass; the sweep does not separate the two.
Combined, the three settings make the step 5.7× faster (+469%).

**All runs below use bf16, compilation and distributed physics.**

## Patch size: limited by the convolution library, not by memory

The volume is cut into cubic tiles that are denoised independently and blended
over their overlap. The patch size sets how many tiles there are and how large
each convolution is: larger tiles mean fewer, larger kernels and less
recomputed overlap.

**The limit is set by MIOpen, not by memory.** Going from patch 64 to 128
halves the step (121 s to 65 s). Peak memory stays between 46 and 53 GB at
every patch size, far below the 128 GB of the APU, yet patch 192 fails: its
tile exceeds the 32-bit index range of the 3D convolution. Patch 176 is a
further 8% faster than 128, but uses 6.5 GB more and does not divide the
1024-voxel axes of the volume.

**The rest of this page uses patch 128.**

## Checkpointing and strong scaling: one volume, more GPUs

The feature maps of a 3D network are sized by the volume, not by the network,
and the backward pass needs all of them. Activation checkpointing discards each
tile's activations in the forward pass and recomputes them in the backward
pass: it trades compute for memory. Spreading the volume over more GPUs also
divides the activations between ranks, and shortens the step. Here the volume is fixed and the number of GPUs varies from 4 to 128,
with checkpointing `always` and `never`.

**Efficiency drops as GPUs are added.** The step falls from 64.8 s on 4 GPUs
to 20.1 s on 64 and 21.0 s on 128; efficiency is 72% at 8 GPUs, 49% at 16, 32%
at 32 and 20% at 64. As on the <a href={trainHref}>training scaling page</a>,
more GPUs means less efficiency. Here it is worse for two reasons: the
collectives move the full 1024×512×1024 volume, whose size does not shrink with
the GPU count, and every run beyond 4 GPUs spans several nodes.

**Against this loss, checkpointing is a small cost.** At 128 GPUs, `never` is
7% faster than `always` (19.6 s against 21.0 s), while the efficiency there is
about 10%. A faster training comes from spreading each volume over fewer GPUs,
with DDP across groups (next section).

Above 41 ranks, some ranks hold no tilt angle, and memory per GPU rises from
42 GB at 32 GPUs to 46 GB at 64. A run on 32 GPUs with the operator restricted
to 16 ranks reproduces the rise.

**Everything below runs with checkpointing `always`.**

## GPUs per volume: tiling groups with data parallelism

Spreading one volume over more GPUs is one way to use a larger machine; the
other is to train on several volumes at once. Every run below tiles the
volume. What changes is how many GPUs share one volume. With $k$ GPUs per
volume, the $N$ GPUs form $N/k$ groups: each group tiles its own volume, and
DDP averages the gradients across groups. $k = N$ is the single-group case of
the previous section, without DDP; $k = 1$ gives each GPU a whole volume, still
processed tile by tile. A step processes one volume per group, so efficiency
is computed on the time per volume.

**Small groups with DDP keep efficiency near 100%.** DDP only all-reduces the
gradients of the U-Net, which are small next to the volume, so adding groups
costs almost nothing. With 4 GPUs per volume, each group stays within one node,
and efficiency stays at 96–99% up to 64 GPUs. Larger groups span several nodes
and carry the losses of the previous section: 66–67% with 8 GPUs per volume,
48–52% with 16, about 31% with 32 and 19% with 64. One, two and four GPUs per
volume perform the same: all stay within one node, where communication is
fast. Values above 100% are run-to-run noise, which depends on the nodes
allocated.

For a fixed number of GPUs, the trade is time per volume against volumes per
step. On 64 GPUs, 4 per volume gives 16 volumes per 65-second step, 4.1 s per
volume; a single group over all 64 gives one volume per 20.6-second step. The
groups of 4 process five times more volumes per second; a single group over
all 64 GPUs only shortens the time of one step, which matters only when there
is a single volume to train on.

## What to actually do

1. **Use bf16 first.** It makes the step 5.3× faster on its own, because the
   step is dominated by 3D convolutions. Compilation and distributed physics
   add 7.5% on top of it, and almost nothing without it.
2. **Use the largest patch that runs and divides the volume.** Larger tiles
   mean fewer, larger convolutions. On MI300A, MIOpen's 32-bit indexing sets
   the limit long before the 128 GB of memory does.
3. **Keep checkpointing on unless the run already spans enough GPUs to fit
   without it.** Dropping it saves 7% of the step at 128 GPUs, but only 128
   GPUs fit it, and there the parallel efficiency is about 10%.
4. **Keep each volume within one node, and add groups with DDP.** The number
   of GPUs per volume follows from the GPUs available, the number of volumes
   wanted per step, and the minimum that fits one volume (a single GPU here,
   with checkpointing). Any choice up to 4 GPUs per volume stays within one
   node and keeps efficiency near 100% up to 64 GPUs; a volume over several
   nodes is bounded by the full-volume collectives between them.
